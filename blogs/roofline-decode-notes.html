memory-bound vs compute-bound

Why decode is memory-bound,
one token at a time

A walk through the roofline model for LLM inference — why the GPU's compute units sit idle during decode, what actually gets moved from HBM every step, and why the fix is to chase the ridge point, not avoid it.

01 The two axes

The roofline model plots what a piece of hardware can actually achieve, as a function of one ratio: how much compute you do per byte you move.

  • x-axis — arithmetic intensity: FLOPs performed ÷ bytes moved from memory.
  • y-axis — achievable throughput: FLOPs per second you actually get.

The chart has two regimes, joined at a corner called the ridge point:

  • Sloped region (left): throughput = bandwidth × intensity. You're capped by how fast bytes arrive.
  • Flat region (right): throughput = peak FLOPs/s. You're capped by how fast the compute units can chew through math.

The ridge point itself is just where those two caps happen to be equal — the intensity at which the hardware's peak bandwidth and peak compute are perfectly balanced.

Interactive · where each phase sits on the roofline
Decode: one token's matvec against the full weights + full KV cache. Intensity is low — the point sits far left of the ridge, deep in the sloped region. Compute finishes almost instantly and idles while bytes keep arriving.

02 What decode actually transfers

To produce one new token, the GPU has to pull two things out of HBM:

  1. The entire model's weights — every layer, all parameters. Fixed size, regardless of how long the conversation is.
  2. The entire KV cache accumulated so far — the keys and values for every previous token, every layer. This one grows as the sequence gets longer.

And what does it compute against all that data? A matrix–vector multiply — one query vector, for one token. Tiny amount of math, relative to the sheer volume of bytes it took to assemble the inputs.

the imbalance Bytes moved: weights + KV cache — large, and growing. FLOPs done: one token's worth of matvecs — small, and roughly constant. Arithmetic intensity is therefore low, which is exactly what places decode on the sloped, memory-bound side of the roofline.

Worked example — a 7B model at 4K context

per-decode-step byte transfer (illustrative, fp16)
model weights (7B params × 2 bytes)≈ 14 GB
KV cache @ 4,096 tokens (32 layers, 32 heads, d=128)≈ 2 GB
total bytes to move, this one step≈ 16 GB
HBM bandwidth (e.g. A100-class)≈ 2 TB/s
→ ≈ 8 ms floor just to move the data — and the matvec compute itself takes a small fraction of that. The GPU spends most of the step waiting, not computing.

Notice the weight term (14 GB) dwarfs the KV term (2 GB) here — but the KV term is the one that keeps climbing.

03 Why it gets worse with sequence length

The weight transfer is a flat tax — same cost whether you're on token 10 or token 10,000. The KV cache transfer is a slope — it grows linearly with every token you've already generated, because every previous token's keys and values have to be re-read from HBM on every subsequent step.

KV cache size vs. sequence length — same 7B config
1,024 tokens≈ 0.5 GB
4,096 tokens≈ 2 GB
32,768 tokens≈ 17 GB
→ past a certain length, the KV cache transfer can rival or exceed the weight transfer — the "flat tax" stops being the dominant cost.

So as a conversation or document gets longer, each new token doesn't just cost the same fixed weight-load anymore — it also drags an ever-larger cache through the memory bus. That's why per-token decode latency creeps up as context grows, even though the compute per token barely changed.

04 The actual goal: climb toward the ridge, don't stay below it

It's tempting to think "stay under the hardware's limits" is the target. It's the opposite: sitting far below the ridge point means compute capacity you already paid for is going unused.

the fix Every step you take toward the ridge point — by raising arithmetic intensity — converts idle compute into throughput, for free, without moving more bytes. Below the ridge, more intensity ⇒ proportionally more throughput. At the ridge, both resources are finally busy at once.

The main lever is batching: load the weights from HBM once, then multiply them against many sequences' query vectors at once instead of just one. Same weight-bytes moved, many more FLOPs performed — intensity rises, and the point on the chart slides right, toward the ridge.

leverwhat it doeseffect on the chart
Batching Amortizes one weight load across many sequences' matvecs. ↑ intensity — slides right toward the ridge
GQA / MQA Fewer KV heads ⇒ smaller cache to read per step. ↓ bytes — same effect, less to move
KV cache quantization Store K/V in INT8/FP8 instead of FP16. ↓ bytes — cache shrinks directly
PagedAttention Efficient, non-wasteful KV memory layout. ↓ waste — more headroom for bigger batches

Batching fixes the weight half of the transfer well, since it's shared across the batch. It does not shrink the KV cache term — each sequence in the batch still carries its own cache, which is why KV cache size, not weight size, is usually the actual ceiling on how large a batch can get.

06 The takeaway

Every decode step re-reads the entire model and the entire conversation-so-far from HBM, just to run one small matvec. Bandwidth, not compute, sets the pace — and the compute units spend the step idle, waiting for bytes. As context grows, the KV cache half of that transfer keeps climbing, so the wait gets longer per token. The way out isn't to avoid the hardware's limits — it's to raise arithmetic intensity until you're using both compute and bandwidth at once, mainly by batching, with cache-shrinking tricks (GQA/MQA, quantization, PagedAttention) buying more room to batch into.
