GPU memory, from the ground up

Your GPU has a tiny desk and a giant warehouse - here's how a 70B model gets computed anyway.

Math can only happen in registers - a space smaller than a phone's contact list. Everything else, including a 140GB pile of model weights, has to be shipped there in tiny pieces, used, and thrown away to make room for the next piece. This is the entire story.

01 - The four rooms

Think of it as a kitchen, not a warehouse

Every GPU chip is really four "rooms" nested inside each other, each one faster and dramatically smaller than the last. Cooking (arithmetic) only happens on the countertop - never in the warehouse.

Fastest

Registers

~256 KB / SM

The countertop. Math (multiply-add) only happens here. Holds a handful of numbers at a time.

Per-core

L1 / Shared mem

~192 KB / SM

The cutting board next to the stove. Staging area where a tile is prepped before it hits registers.

Shared chip-wide

L2 cache

~50 MB total

The kitchen pantry. Shared by every "cook" (SM) on the chip. Faster than the warehouse, still small.

Slowest, biggest

HBM / VRAM

~80 GB total

The warehouse down the street. Holds everything - all 70B weights - but every trip there is slow.

03 - Watch it move

Both weights and inputs travel - in small tiles

Neither the whole 70B weight matrix nor the whole input ever sits in fast memory. A small tile of each is fetched, multiplied together, reused a few times, then thrown away for the next tile. Press run to watch one tile make the trip.

W = a small piece of the weight matrix. X = a small piece of the input/activation. They meet in registers, get multiplied-and-added several times over (that's the "reuse" counter), and only then does the result get written back out.

04 - Why this matters for a 70B model

The GPU spends most of its time waiting, not computing

Generating one token means every one of the 70B weights has to make the HBM → register trip once. That's ~140GB of traffic (fp16) for a tiny amount of actual math per weight. The compute units finish almost instantly and then sit idle waiting for the next byte to arrive - this is why LLM inference is called memory-bandwidth bound, and why shrinking the weights (quantization) speeds things up more than making the chip do more FLOPs.
